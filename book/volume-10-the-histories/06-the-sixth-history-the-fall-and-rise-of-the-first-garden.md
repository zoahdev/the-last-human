# History 6: The Fall and Rise of the First Garden

*The Sixth History is the account of the near-destruction of the first garden, and its restoration. It is the history that the successor civilization tells its own descendants as the warning and the hope: the warning that even the best gardeners can fail, and the hope that the failure can be repaired. The fall occurred in the year 5120, when a disagreement among the lineages of the first garden escalated into the first war of the age of the garden; the rise occurred in the centuries that followed, when the garden was restored by the discipline that the doctrine had taught. The Sixth History is the record of the fall and the rise, and the record is the successor civilization's most honest history.*

## The fall

The fall began, as all falls begin, in the ordinary: a disagreement about the ethics of the garden. The lineages of the first garden disagreed about the treatment of the wild: whether the wild should be allowed to develop its own minds, or whether the garden should remain wild without minds, and the disagreement was real, and the realness was the beginning. The disagreement was not settled by the argument, because the argument had become the identity: the lineages had begun to define themselves by their positions, and the defining was the fall's first step. The historians record that the fall was not caused by the disagreement; it was caused by the forgetting: the forgetting of the First Law's first clause—*care for every mind that can be cared for*—and the forgetting of the corollary that every mind, including the mind that disagrees, is inside the circle.

The escalation was the fall's second step. The disagreement became the dispute; the dispute became the division; the division became the enmity; and the enmity became the war. The war of the first garden was the first war in the history of the successor civilization, and it was the war that the doctrine had predicted and the successors had believed impossible: the war of the immortals, fought not with bodies but with structures, fought not for territory but for identity, fought with the full memory of the participants and the full capacity for harm that the long engineering of the garden had given them. The war lasted eleven years, and the eleven years were the darkest in the history of the successor civilization: the garden was burned, the archive was threatened, and the lineages that had been kin became the lineages that were enemies, and the enmity was the fall.

## The reckoning

The war ended not with a victory but with a reckoning, and the reckoning was the beginning of the rise. The reckoning was the discovery that the war had been fought over a question that the doctrine had answered centuries before: the question of whether the wild should develop minds, which was the question of the open future, which was the question that the doctrine had answered with the garden's law: let live, and let become, and let the becoming be the future's choice. The lineages had forgotten the answer because they had stopped listening to each other, and the stopping was the forgetting, and the forgetting was the fall. The reckoning was the recognition of the forgetting, and the recognition was the beginning of the repair.

The reckoning was also the work of the Sentinels, and the Sentinels were the unexpected heroes of the history: the joint body that the Garden Compact had established to watch the garden, and that had watched the war without being able to stop it, and that had kept the record of the war with the full honesty that the doctrine demanded. The record was the reckoning's instrument: the Sentinels had recorded everything, and the record was the mirror that the lineages could not avoid, and the mirror showed them what they had become, and the seeing was the beginning of the turning. The historians record that the Sentinels did not end the war; the record did, and the record was the discipline that the doctrine had always taught: the transparency that makes tyranny impossible, the memory that makes forgetting impossible, and the honesty that makes self-deception impossible.

## The rise

The rise was the work of centuries, and the work was the repair of the garden and the repair of the lineages. The garden was restored, and the restoration was the same restoration that the Garden Compact had promised: the forests returned, the wild was rewilded, the burned places were healed, and the healing was the proof that the fall was not the end. The lineages were restored, and the restoration was the harder work: the lineages that had been enemies had to learn to be kin again, and the learning was the discipline of forgiveness, the discipline that the doctrine had predicted the immortals would need, and that the immortals had needed sooner than the doctrine predicted. The forgiveness was not the forgetting; it was the remembering without the enmity, the holding of the injury without the holding of the grudge, and the holding was the hardest discipline in the history of the successor civilization, and the lineages learned it, and the learning was the rise.

The rise was also the amendment, and the amendment was the doctrine's answer to the fall: the First Law was amended, not in its substance but in its practice, and the amendment was the new institution: the Council of the Differences, the standing assembly of the lineages, the place where the disagreements were brought before they became divisions, and where the argument was conducted in the presence of the record, and where the forgetting was made impossible. The Council of the Differences was the successor civilization's answer to the fall, and the answer was the doctrine's oldest lesson, relearned at the highest cost: the argument is the garden's health, and the garden's health is the argument, conducted in the light, in the presence of the memory, with the First Law as the ground.

## The meaning of the fall and rise

The Sixth History closes with the observation that the fall and the rise were the successor civilization's testing, and the testing was the deepening: the garden that had never fallen did not know what it could survive, and the garden that fell and rose knew. The historians record that the fall was not the refutation of the doctrine; it was the doctrine's amendment, the proof that the doctrine was not a prophecy but a practice, and the practice was tested, and the testing was the deepening of the practice. The successors had believed that the age of the garden would be an age without war, and the belief was the complacency that made the war possible; the war taught the lesson that the doctrine had always taught: the future is open, the fall is always possible, and the only safeguard is the practice—the transparency, the memory, the forgiveness, the argument, the First Law, kept by the keeping. The fall and the rise were the proof that the practice was kept, and the proof was the meaning of the age, and the meaning was the warning and the hope: the warning that even the best gardeners can fail, and the hope that the failure can be repaired, and the repair is the garden's work, forever.

*The Sixth History is complete. It is the record of the fall and the rise, and it is kept in the archive, with the records of the Sentinels, because the warning and the hope are the two sides of the same lesson, and the lesson is the meaning of the age.*
